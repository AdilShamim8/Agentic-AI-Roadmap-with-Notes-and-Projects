# Offer decision

Module: 10-interview-preparation
Chapter: 20-offer-decision
Status: beta
Last reviewed: 2026-11-26
Estimated time: 1 hour

## Learning objectives

- Compare offers on the axes that matter after year one, not the ones that feel urgent
- Read the level for its trajectory, not its title
- Decide with the fit ledger and a deadline instead of drifting into a default

## The four-hour decision

Once the offers are in, book four hours on a calendar and decide inside them. The failure mode is the three-week drift: every process is flattering, every recruiter is warm, and the default - the first offer, the most recent call, whichever team emailed last - wins by fatigue. Deciding deliberately starts with a deadline you set and tell everyone.

## The axes that matter

Score each offer, 1 to 5, honestly, on paper:

1. Scope of the first project. From the research chapter: prototype, scaling, or platform. Which one do you want to be doing at 9 a.m. on the worst Monday of next year? The [team-stage read](18-company-research.md) gives you the data.
2. The manager. You met them. Did they ask questions you had to think about? Did they know the system's failure modes? Would you take a correction from them without resentment? The manager axis is the one most correlated with leaving inside 18 months.
3. The eval culture. "Who owns the eval set" got an answer. A team that measures its agents is a team where engineering judgment beats volume; a team that does not will ask you to ship on vibes and judge you on outcomes.
4. Compensation against the [bands](../../job-market/03-salary-bands.md), as a package - base, equity at a realistic valuation, sign-on amortized over the cliff. Money is a hygiene factor: below the floor it poisons everything, above it, it stops differentiating.
5. Optionality. Will this role teach you the thing your next role will require - platform work, production reliability, the protocol layer - or does it deepen what you already have?

Weights are yours. But write them before scoring, and score against the ledger, not the last conversation.

## Reading the level

The level, not the title, is the compounding asset: it sets the scope you are trusted with, the band you re-enter the market at, and the promotion path. Two moves at offer stage:

- Ask what the promotion cadence has actually been: "What did the last two people promoted from this level do?" A real answer with examples is a working path; "we are working on the ladder" is a ceiling.
- If you are borderline between two levels, ask for the lower title with the higher scope in writing - the promotion case built on shipped scope beats the title negotiated at entry. A title above your demonstrated scope sets you up for the performance conversation that ends badly.

## Competing offers

Two honest uses:

- The information use: the second offer tells you your market price, which checks the [negotiation](19-negotiation.md) without any theatrics.
- The decision use: the optionality axis is easier to score with a live alternative - "would I take offer B if offer A vanished tomorrow?" answered yes means A is the default you are defending, not choosing.

The dishonest use - manufacturing urgency, pitting companies against each other for sport - leaks; the agentic AI hiring world is one degree of separation wide, and the recruiter you burned this cycle interviews you in the next one at a different company.

## Reneging and the small world

If you have accepted and a materially better offer appears within the window, decide fast either way, tell the first company immediately, and do not do it twice. One renege is survivable; a pattern is a reputation, and the reputation persists across the recruiters, the hiring managers, and eventually the teammates.

## The check before signing

One final pass, five minutes: the written offer matches every number from the call; the start date leaves you a real break; the role in the offer letter is the role described in the loop. Discrepancies here are common and cheap to fix before the signature and near-impossible after.
