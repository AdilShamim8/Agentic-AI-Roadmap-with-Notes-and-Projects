# Questions for the panel

Module: 10-interview-preparation
Chapter: 27-questions-for-panel
Status: beta
Last reviewed: 2026-11-26
Estimated time: 30 minutes

## Learning objectives

- Use "any questions for us?" as the easiest scoring window in the loop
- Read the team's real stage and health from the answers
- Retire the questions that signal nothing

## Why this round inside the round

Every interviewer ends with "what questions do you have?" The question block is graded, lightly, on curiosity and preparation - and more importantly, it is your only data-collection window inside the loop. Two questions per round, matched to who is asking. Going in with none reads as low interest in this job specifically; going in with the same generic two for every interviewer reads as scripted.

## Questions by interviewer

The recruiter: "How many rounds, and who runs the technical ones?" and "What is the timeline from here?" Scheduling information you need anyway, asked at the right moment.

The hiring manager: "What does the first 90 days look like?" and "What is the state of the agent system - prototype, in production, or between?" The second answer is the [stage read](18-company-research.md) from the source, and it recalibrates every remaining story you tell.

The engineer: "Who owns the eval set, and when did the regression gate last catch something?" and "What is the on-call story when the agent breaks at 2 a.m.?" Engineers answer these honestly; the answers are the culture, unfiltered.

The executive or founder: "What is the AI bet the company has actually made - budget and headcount, not press release?" and "What would make you say in a year this hire was a mistake?" The second question sounds aggressive and lands as seriousness; the answer tells you how the success of the role is measured.

## Reading the answers

- "We are still figuring out the eval question" from an engineer, at a company whose product is an agent in production: prototype instincts at scaling stage. Good role if you want to build the practice; bad role if you expect one.
- "The gate caught two regressions last month," with specifics: an eval culture that exists, not one that is claimed. The strongest single answer you can hear.
- On-call answers that name a rotation, a runbook, and a recent incident they fixed: a team that operates its system. An answer about "the platform team handles that": a team that ships and hopes.
- "The first 90 days" answered with a project, not "ramping": scope exists, and the role has a shape. The ramping answer is fine at a big company and a warning at a startup.

## The questions that signal nothing

Retire these: "What is the culture like?" (answered by everyone with the same three adjectives); "What do you like about working here?" (invites the same answer from everyone); anything answerable from the website's first page. Also retire the compensation questions in the technical rounds - that window belongs to the recruiter screen and the offer call ([negotiation](19-negotiation.md) timeline), and asking it of engineers marks the calendar wrong.

## The one question to keep in your pocket

End every final round with: "What would the strongest person in this role do in the first month that a merely good one would not?" It is the entire rubric, handed to you - the answer names the axis the team actually optimizes for, and your thank-you note can reference it in one sentence, which is the last impression you control.
