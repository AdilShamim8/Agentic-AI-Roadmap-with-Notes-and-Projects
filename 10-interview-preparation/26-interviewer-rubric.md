# Interviewer rubric

Module: 10-interview-preparation
Chapter: 26-interviewer-rubric
Status: beta
Last reviewed: 2026-11-26
Estimated time: 1 hour

## Learning objectives

- Score your own rounds the way the debrief scores them
- Use the self-scoring sheet after every mock and every real round
- Know which rubric axes are pass/fail and which are gradients

## The axes

Most panels file feedback on four to five axes. The names vary; the content does not. Score each 1 to 5 after a round, honestly, with one sentence of evidence per score - the evidence sentence is the part that makes the score real.

1. Technical depth. Can the candidate explain the systems they claim, at the mechanism level - state, reducers, tools, persistence, evaluation - under a follow-up? Gradient axis: 3 is "explains own work soundly," 5 is "teaches the interviewer something."
2. System design. Requirements first, architecture with named alternatives, evaluation volunteered, production numbers, failure modes. Gradient axis - and the one with the most room between prepared and unprepared candidates.
3. Communication. Answers land inside two minutes, at the block level, out loud, without hedging or drowning. Gradient, but with a floor: below 3 on communication caps every other axis in practice, because the panel can only score what it can hear.
4. Ownership signal. Stories with decisions, costs, numbers, and aftermath; failures owned; the "not my memory" pattern from [the behavioral mock](23-mock-behavioral.md). Gradient, and the axis hiring managers weight heaviest.
5. Red flags. Binary. Invents facts, blames teammates, dismisses evaluation, hides not-knowing. A single red flag can convert four passing axes into a no-hire - the debrief treats them as risk, and risk vetoes.

## The self-scoring sheet

After every mock and every real round, five minutes:

- Axis 1: score, evidence sentence.
- Axis 2: score, evidence.
- Axis 3: score, evidence.
- Axis 4: score, evidence.
- Red flags: count, each with its exact trigger sentence.
- One fix for the lowest axis, one sentence.

Keep the sheets in the same log as the sprint debrief ([week 3](24-four-week-sprint.md)). Across five rounds the pattern is unmistakable: most candidates have one axis at 3-minus that every sheet keeps flagging, and that axis is the next hour of preparation. The sheets exist to find it - the aggregate is the value, not any single round.

## Pass/fail lines worth knowing

- The eval answer. In this field, "how would you measure this" unanswered is treated as a gap, not a style choice. The [design framework](05-system-design-structure.md) budgets eight minutes for it for exactly this reason.
- The non-running artifact. The take-home that fails to run from a fresh clone does not get graded on its ideas.
- The invented number. A statistic you cannot source, pressed once, converts the whole answer - and often the round - into a red-flag note. If you do not know, the bounded "here is where my knowledge ends" from [the landscape chapter](01-interview-landscape.md) is the move.
- The integrity beat. In agent-heavy loops, "the human approval gate is structural, not prompt-level" has quietly become a pass/fail line: it separates candidates who have shipped agent systems from candidates who have read about them.

## Calibrating against the real thing

After a real round, score yourself before any feedback arrives, then compare with what the recruiter relays weeks later. The calibration gap is itself the skill: candidates who score themselves within a point of the panel's verdict are ready to iterate without waiting for rejections; candidates two points optimistic keep being surprised by the no.
